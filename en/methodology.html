How we calculate waiting times

When Gasolina says "1 h 20 min", that is not a single number we read somewhere. It is a decision between three kinds of source that measure different things and often disagree. This page describes that decision in full — including the cases where the site deliberately refuses to give a number.

Last updated: 12 August 2026.

Three kinds of source

Everything that reaches the site falls into one of three groups. What separates them is not accuracy but what was measured in the first place.

KindWhoWhat it actually gives
Official estimate Serbian Border Police (published via AMSS), police.hu, AMSM An estimate of total delay at the crossing. Not a measurement. The Hungarian police say so explicitly — their figures are "based on experience and informative in nature".
Driver report BorderAlarm, the form on this site How long one person actually stood there. Closest to the truth when it is right, but the sample is tiny and nobody verifies it.
Traffic measurement TomTom (delay and queue length on the corridor up to the barrier) The only real measurement — but it only sees vehicles that are moving.

The rule everything rests on: a lower bound may confirm, never refute

Traffic measurement looks like the strongest source because it is the only automatic one. In practice it is the weakest at the thing we care about. trafficDelayInSeconds measures how much slower traffic moves than it would on an empty road. A queue standing at passport control does not exist for that system — no movement, no delay.

So a zero from traffic measurement means three different things that cannot be told apart: the road really is clear · the road is not covered by measurement · a four-hour queue is standing at the end of it.

This is not a theoretical worry. Across an archive of 475 data snapshots (24 June – 3 August 2026, 11,574 records by crossing and direction) two corridors never once produced a positive signal: the inbound corridor at Bogorodica across 100 successful responses, and at Medzitlija across 94 — every single one a zero. Those corridors are dead, and the rule that treated zero as evidence was running there too.

Until August 2026 a measurement was allowed to overrule a driver report: if someone reported four hours while the measurement looked calm, the report disappeared from the display. When we replayed that backwards across the whole archive, it turned out the site did not print "No queue" in those cases — it did not lie, it went silent. You got a page with no number at all, in exactly the situation where one source had a very definite opinion. A milder fault than we first thought, but the same kind.

Today the rule runs one way only: measurement may confirm congestion, never disprove it. A measured 700-metre queue still overrides a reassuring headline, because confirming is what a lower bound is entitled to do. The absence of a queue overrides nothing — it only means there is no confirmation.

What each label claims

LabelWhat it claims
No queueAn official source says there is no delay, or under 15 minutes. This requires positive evidence — a single short driver report is not enough.
A figure (e.g. 45 min)An official estimate, with the source named.
Reported + figureThe figure comes from drivers, not from an institution. The word sits next to the number, not in small print underneath.
Queue ~800 mMeasurement confirmed the length of the line. Length is not duration and is never converted into minutes.
Sources disagreeTwo estimates differ by more than a factor of three. The site shows both and picks no winner.
UncertainDrivers report a long wait and there is no confirmation — neither from an institution nor from measurement. The report is visible, but never becomes the headline figure.
No reliable estimateNo source said anything usable. This is an answer, not a malfunction.

"Sources disagree" and "Uncertain" deliberately carry no single headline number. The moment one number exists, every display prints it as the headline and thereby declares a winner — and the whole point is that there is no winner.

Why AMSS's exact 30 minutes never becomes a figure

In the archive, the value exactly 30 minutes accounted for 6,232 of 6,404 records from that source — 97.3%. A value that shows up in nineteen cases out of twenty is not an assessment of conditions; it is the value that stands until a change is reported. So an exact 30 does not enter the decision: in that case the page says there is no reliable estimate and points you at the camera.

An important limit on that claim: AMSS can publish a finer value — we found 15 minutes at Ljubovija–Bratunac and Nakovo, in both directions. So 30 is not their floor, it is the value they hold. No crossing this site tracks has ever published 15, so this correction changes not a single displayed figure — but it changes the reasoning, and that is why it is written here.

For comparison: the Hungarian police publish only four values (0, 30, 45 and 60 minutes), while AMSM uses a finer scale (15, 20, 30, 40, 45, 50, 60) and reports zero in 85.2% of records. The granularity of a source is part of what its number means.

What the site does not know

  • The camera and the number need not describe the same point. When the source is the North Macedonian or Hungarian side, that is the wait at their control — downstream of a camera pointed at the Serbian apron. An empty apron in the picture does not refute such a number.
  • Traffic measurement does not cover every direction. Of 26 directions (13 crossings × 2), ten have no corridor at all — among them Spiljani and Sremska Raca in both directions.
  • Driver reports are not verified. One report is one person. That is why a single short report never yields "No queue", and a single extreme one never yields a headline figure without confirmation.
  • We do not forecast. The site says what the sources are reporting now, not how long you will wait when you get there.

How this is kept from breaking

The waiting decision lives in one file used by every page. Until August 2026 the same logic sat in four copies — and the home page showed 34 minutes for a crossing while that crossing's own page showed 20, because one copy was left untouched twice when the rule changed.

Every rule change passes three layers of checks before it goes live:

  1. Rules — seventeen checks, each written after a real incident. They are not removed to make a test pass.
  2. Frozen behaviour — 1,046 unique input combinations drawn from the archive. If any result changes, the change must be deliberate and written down, never silent.
  3. Display — 29 pages driven through nine situations in a real browser, checking that none of them prints a blank or a claim the data does not support.

On top of that, every rule change is replayed backwards across the whole archive to see how many records it would move. The most recent one (removing the measurement veto) changed 323 of 11,574 records — 2.79% — and not one of them gained a new unconfirmed headline figure.

If you see a figure that does not match what is actually happening, tell us: kontakt@gasolina.rs. The time, the crossing and the direction are what help most — with those we can find the exact record in the archive and check which source got it wrong.
